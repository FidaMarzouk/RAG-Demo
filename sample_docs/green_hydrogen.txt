Hydrogen can serve as a fuel and as a way to store energy, but how clean it is depends on how it is made. Most hydrogen produced today comes from natural gas through steam methane reforming and is called "grey" hydrogen because the process releases carbon dioxide. "Blue" hydrogen uses the same process but captures and stores most of that carbon dioxide. "Green" hydrogen is produced by electrolysis, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen using electricity from renewable sources, so it involves no direct carbon emissions.

The device that performs electrolysis is called an electrolyzer. The main types are alkaline electrolyzers, the most established and lowest-cost technology; proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolyzers, which respond quickly to changing power input and therefore suit variable solar and wind supply; and solid oxide electrolyzers, which operate at high temperatures and can reach higher efficiency, especially when waste heat is available.

Converting electricity to hydrogen and back to electricity loses a large share of the energy: electrolysis is roughly 60 to 80 percent efficient, and the full power-to-hydrogen-to-power round trip typically returns only about 30 to 40 percent of the original electricity. For that reason green hydrogen is mainly targeted at uses that are hard to electrify directly, such as steel making, producing ammonia for fertilizer, and long-distance heavy transport, as well as seasonal energy storage. Large volumes of hydrogen can be stored underground in salt caverns.
