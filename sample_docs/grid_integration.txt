Integrating large amounts of variable renewable energy into the electric grid requires keeping supply and demand balanced at every moment. Grid operators rely on several tools: weather-based forecasting of solar and wind output, flexible "dispatchable" generation such as hydropower or gas turbines that can ramp up and down, energy storage, demand response programs that pay large consumers to shift or reduce their electricity use, and transmission lines that connect distant regions so that a calm or cloudy area can import power from a windy or sunny one.

A plant's capacity factor is its actual output over a period divided by the output it would have produced running at full power the whole time. Because they depend on weather, solar farms typically reach capacity factors of about 10 to 25 percent and onshore wind farms about 25 to 45 percent, with offshore wind often at the top of that range or above it. Geothermal and nuclear plants, which run continuously, reach much higher values.

When there is more renewable generation than the grid can use or transmit, operators must curtail it, meaning some solar or wind output is deliberately wasted. In regions with a lot of solar power, net demand (total demand minus solar output) drops sharply in the middle of the day and rises steeply in the evening as the sun sets. Plotted over a day, this shape resembles a duck and is known as the "duck curve", a term popularized by California's grid operator.

Traditional power plants also stabilize the grid through the inertia of their large spinning generators, which slows changes in frequency after a sudden failure. Solar panels and batteries connect through power electronics called inverters and provide no natural inertia; newer grid-forming inverters can imitate this stabilizing behavior.
